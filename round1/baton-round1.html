At a glance

Executive summary

When someone leaves a project, the documents usually stay behind. What leaves with them is the reasoning: why a decision was made, what is half-finished, who to call, and which problem keeps coming back. Baton rebuilds that reasoning from the project's authorised sources. It shows exactly where each statement came from, says clearly what it doesn't know, and turns the result into a continuation plan that a responsible person reviews and approves before the newcomer sees it.

The insight

The usual answer is an AI summary. A summary can only restate what's already written down, and it sounds just as sure of itself when it's guessing. The knowledge that's actually at risk is the part that was never written down. So Baton is built around finding gaps first: it works out what the sources can't support, and spends the leaver's last days filling those gaps.

The product

There are seven steps: Connect → Reconstruct → Verify → Ask → Review → Handover → Continue. The result is a handover tailored to each reader's role. It contains typed, cited claims, a timeline of decisions, a map of who owns what, a list of risks and dependencies, and a Day 1 / Week 1 / Month 1 plan that the incoming person signs off on.

Why it's feasible

The MVP needs just two kinds of source: the GitHub API, plus uploaded files (Slack/Discord exports, documents, meeting transcripts, task-board CSVs). For the demo we'll use a synthetic project with a known answer key, so we can measure accuracy and leakage instead of just claiming them.

§1 · Understanding of the problem

Projects don't lose documents. They lose the reasoning.

A departing member usually leaves behind a repository, a drive folder and a long chat history. What the incoming person actually needs is spread across all of them and rarely written down: why things are the way they are, what's half-done, and what will go wrong next.

Why existing documentation and tools fail

  • Docs capture what, rarely why. Reasoning happens in conversation, and conversation is the part nobody curates.
  • Handover notes get written at the worst moment: in the last week, by someone busy and already checked out. They suffer from the curse of knowledge, which means the writer can't see what a newcomer doesn't know.
  • Docs go stale quietly. A wiki page written in January says nothing about the April reversal, and doesn't warn you that it's out of date.
  • Generic AI summarisers sound equally sure when they're right and when they're guessing. They tidy up contradictions instead of flagging them, and they can only summarise what was written. They never ask what's missing.
  • Search tools answer the questions you know to ask. A newcomer's biggest risks are the questions they don't know to ask yet.
  • Permissions often stop at retrieval. A summary can mix restricted facts (budget, HR, credentials) into text that goes to everyone.

The part of the problem we will solve

We focus on planned and sudden departures from small and mid-sized project teams whose work lives in a code repository plus chat, documents, meeting notes and a task board. These are student tech clubs, hackathon and open-source teams, research groups and early-stage software teams. Our goal is to shorten the time it takes the incoming person to work safely and independently, without ever showing them anything they aren't authorised to see. We are deliberately not building a general enterprise search engine, or a tool that judges the leaver's performance.

§2 · Users affected

Four people sit around every handover

Our first market is college technical clubs and student project teams. Their leadership turns over almost completely every year at graduation, they run on free tools (GitHub, Slack/Discord, Google Docs, Trello/Notion), and they have no knowledge-management staff at all. Environments

Baton is a browser-based web app with no installation, so it runs on any laptop. Sources are connected through OAuth (the GitHub App, which only reads) or by uploading exports, which suits teams that can't grant live access. Hosting is in the cloud for the hackathon. §3 · Proposed solution

Baton: a handover built from evidence, gaps and permissions

Baton doesn't write one long summary. It builds an Evidence Ledger, which is a structured set of small claims, each tied to exact source spans. Every handover is put together from that ledger, and its gaps are filled by the one person who can fill them.

1  Evidence Ledger with trust states

An extraction model reads each source and pulls out small claims of 8 types: Decision, Ownership, Open Question, Risk, Dependency, Blocker / Recurring Problem, Next Action, Contact. Each claim points to exact character spans in its sources. A separate verifier model then checks whether those spans actually support the claim. Every claim ends up with exactly one visible trust state:

VERIFIED INFERRED CONFLICTING UNSUPPORTED / GAP ATTESTED SEALED

An assumption can never look like a fact. Only Verified and Attested (confirmed by a named person, with a date) claims are shown as plain statements. Everything else is labelled where it appears.

2  Gap Interview (our key idea)

Baton looks for what the evidence can't explain: decisions with no recorded reason, tasks with no owner, contradictions, risks with no mitigation, and areas that only the leaver ever touched. It ranks these by risk and turns the top 10–15 into short, specific questions for the leaver, for example: "PR #142 switched auth to college SSO. Why, and what still has to be done before the March certificate renewal?"

Answers come in as typed text (voice is planned for Round 3) and are stored as ATTESTED evidence with the person's name and the date. Twenty minutes answering targeted questions replaces days of writing on a blank page.

3  Sealed Claims: permissions applied when each handover is built

Every span of evidence keeps the access list of the source it came from. When we build a handover for a particular reader, a claim is included only if (a) that reader can open at least one of its sources, (b) the claim still passes verification using only those sources, and (c) its sensitivity level is within the reader's clearance. If a claim fails these checks but matters, the reader sees a locked placeholder in its place: 🔒 1 finance item — request access from Treasurer. The reader knows something is there, and none of its content leaks.

4  Sole-Knower Index and Readiness Score

For each area (a module, a vendor, a process), Baton measures how much of the activity came from the leaver alone. This works like a per-topic "bus factor": how many people would have to leave before nobody understands that area. Combined with how many critical gaps are still open, it gives a Handover Readiness Score (0–100) that the reviewer can watch go up as gaps are answered and claims are approved. This turns "is the handover good enough?" into a number.

§4 · Complete product workflow

From connected sources to an approved, acknowledged handover

The five steps the brief requires are marked with a dark bar. The two amber steps are our additions, and they are what makes Baton more than a summariser.

01
ConnectAdmin starts a handover: leaver, recipients, roles, last day. They connect GitHub or upload exports, and a consent screen shows exactly what will be read.
02
ReconstructSources are split into evidence units (with author, time, access list and link) and redacted. Claims are extracted, and Baton builds the timeline, ownership map and dependency graph.
03
VerifyThe verifier checks each claim against its source spans. Baton applies trust states, finds conflicts and reversed decisions, and computes the Sole-Knower Index.
04
AskThe Gap Interview: the leaver answers the ranked gap questions. Answers become Attested evidence, and the Readiness Score goes up.
05
ReviewThe leaver and the approver accept, edit or reject each claim, resolve conflicts and mark items sensitive. Then they approve a version.
06
HandoverA separate handover is built for each recipient, with Sealed Claims where needed. The share link requires sign-in, and the recipient acknowledges each critical item.
07
ContinueThe recipient works through the plan. New questions get answered from the approved handover, and anything it can't answer goes to the leaver or another owner.

The visibility rule

visible(c, r) ⇔ Er = {e ∈ E(c) : r ∈ acl(e)} ≠ ∅
  ∧ verify(c, Er) = supports
  ∧ sensitivity(c) ≤ clearance(r)
otherwise → SEALED (or hidden if Restricted)

In words: reader r sees claim c only if r can open at least one of c's sources, those sources alone still support c, and c's sensitivity is within r's clearance. The citations shown are only the ones r can open. This rule is enforced in three places: the handover builder, Postgres RLS, and a CI test that fails the build if any planted secret reaches an unauthorised role.

Security

  • The GitHub App asks only for read scopes, and tokens are encrypted when stored
  • Uploads are stored encrypted; a "delete handover" option erases the raw data
  • Share links require sign-in, expire, and can be revoked
  • Append-only audit log of every view and edit

Responsible design

  • The leaver gives consent and can review everything that's attributed to them
  • Baton is not a performance or surveillance tool: it has no per-person productivity metrics
  • We use API settings where providers don't train on our data
  • Built with India's DPDP Act 2023 in mind (purpose limitation, deletion) [R3]

AI reliability

  • Structured output is checked against a schema; malformed output is retried, then dropped
  • The model that verifies claims is separate from the one that extracted them
  • A fixed benchmark is re-run on every change to prompts or models
  • When evidence is missing, Baton says "not in evidence" instead of guessing

How a solo builder keeps this feasible

Use managed services, not infrastructure

Supabase provides the database, authentication, Row-Level Security and file storage in one place, and Vercel hosts the front end. Almost no time goes into running servers, so nearly all of it goes into the parts that make Baton different: the Ledger, the Gap Interview and Sealed Claims.

Build in vertical slices

Each sprint ends with the full workflow running end to end, even if it's rough at first. A judge can try the product at any point, and nothing important is left to integrate at the last minute.

A fixed cut order

If time runs short, features are dropped in the order listed in §5. The core path (Ledger → Verify → Ask → Review → role-based handover) is never cut.

§9 · Expected impact

Measured, not just claimed

The final evaluation principle rewards trustworthy limitations over unsupported claims. So we'll test Baton against a benchmark we design to have a known answer key, and with real users doing timed tasks.

Project Orion benchmark (synthetic)

A synthetic 3-month history of a 6-person club project: about 150 artefacts (commits, PRs, issues, a Slack export, 6 meeting transcripts, 3 docs and a Trello CSV). We plant ~50 ground-truth facts in it:

  • 12 decisions, 3 of them later reversed
  • 6 contradictions across sources
  • 8 risks and 6 dependencies
  • 8 sole-knower facts that exist only in the leaver's head (and should come up as Gap questions)
  • 6 confidential items (sponsor amount, a fake API key, an HR note) that must never reach the Volunteer view

Who benefits and how

Incoming member

Can start contributing in days rather than weeks, knows which landmines to avoid, and can see clearly what is still unknown.

Departing member

Spends about 20 minutes answering questions and correcting a draft instead of writing pages, and leaves with their work properly credited.

Organisation

Keeps sponsor relationships, credentials and context through every graduation, and can track a Readiness Score for each handover.

Ecosystem

Student clubs, NGOs and open-source projects get an option that's free and puts privacy first. These groups have the most turnover and the fewest tools today.

All targets above are hypotheses we'll test in Round 2 and report honestly in Round 3, including any targets we miss.

§10 · Future scope after the event

From one-off handovers to continuous protection

1 · Always-ready handovers

Baton runs all the time, not only when someone resigns. Teams get an always-current handover for every key person and an organisation-wide knowledge-risk heatmap that shows where only one person understands something, before anyone leaves.

2 · A permission-aware knowledge layer for AI assistants

Approved handovers become available to other AI assistants through an MCP server, with the same Sealed Claims rule. Any assistant could then answer "why did we do this?" from evidence a human has approved.

3 · More sources and more settings

Microsoft Teams, Confluence, Notion, Linear, email (with consent), and Google Meet transcripts. Also new settings: research labs (when a PhD student graduates), NGOs (volunteer rotation) and open-source maintainers handing over a project.

4 · Self-hosted, privacy-first edition

A Docker deployment that runs entirely on an institution's own servers, with an option to use local open-weight models. It would be aligned with the DPDP Act, and suits colleges, government projects and regulated industries.

Sustainability model

A free tier for student clubs, NGOs and open-source projects, a paid team plan for startups and small and mid-sized businesses (more sources, longer history, SSO), and a self-hosted licence for institutions. The open evaluation benchmark (Project Orion) will be released publicly so other teams can measure their handover tools the same way.

Known limitations (stated up front)

  • Baton can only reconstruct what was recorded somewhere, or what the leaver attests to. Knowledge from purely verbal conversations that nobody remembers can't be recovered. Baton will flag those gaps, but it can't fill them.
  • Permissions copied from export files (e.g. Slack JSON) are only as accurate as the export. Admins confirm them when uploading.
  • The verifier reduces errors but doesn't remove them. That's why human approval is required, not optional.

Appendix A · Primary research plan

I'm interviewing people on both sides of real handovers in Pune college clubs and small teams, and running a short survey. Results will be reported in Round 2.

Interview guide (20 min)

  • Tell us about the last time you took over (or handed over) a project.
  • What did you only find out after the previous person had left?
  • Where did the missing information actually turn out to be?
  • What would you never want shared with a successor or volunteer?
  • How long did it take until you felt confident making changes?

Survey (target n ≥ 40)

  • Weeks to feel productive after taking over (scale)
  • Hours the leaver spent on handover (scale)
  • Have you ever lost access to credentials, contacts or decisions? (Y/N + example)
  • Rank the most damaging losses: rationale, unfinished work, contacts, credentials, recurring issues
  • Would you trust an AI handover if every claim were cited and reviewed? (Likert)
Status: interviews and the survey are under way. Findings, including the number of respondents and any results that change the plan, will be reported in the Round 2 document.
